## What magnetism does to a mechanical movement

Oris states the problem plainly on its Calibre 400 page: **"Swiss watch movements can magnetise, affecting accuracy."**

A mechanical [movement](/en/glossary/movement/) contains fine metal components — among them the [hairspring](/en/glossary/hairspring/) of the [escapement](/en/mechanisms/escapement/). How strongly a given movement is affected depends on its design, which is why no "typical watch withstands X" figure is quoted here — manufacturers publish different figures for their own constructions.

The Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie (FHH) defines **anti-magnetic** as describing "a watch that is protected against magnetic fields".

## Resistance is a specification of each calibre, each certification

Because there is no universal figure, the reliable way to read anti-magnetic claims is to look at the specification of **each calibre** or **each certification**:

- **One calibre's figure:** Oris states on its Calibre 400 page that the calibre is engineered with **over 30 non-ferrous, anti-magnetic components**, and that it deviated **less than 10 seconds a day after exposure to 2,250 gauss** — which Oris describes as surpassing ISO 764 standards. That is the published specification of that calibre.
- **One certification's criterion:** METAS operates the **MASTER CHRONOMETER** certification, which covers "resistance to magnetic fields" among its criteria. The requirement document for this certification specifies **1.5 T (15,000 gauss)**. According to METAS, the certification is open to **all brands** whose Swiss Made watches carry a movement certified as a chronometer under ISO 3159.
- **One maker's published construction:** IWC states in its March 2023 press release for the Ingenieur Automatic 40: "All new models feature **soft-iron inner cases** to protect the movements from magnetic fields", and describes this construction as one that "efficiently protects the movement from the effects of magnetic fields on its accuracy". This is a construction IWC publishes for its own models — not a shared anti-magnetic level.

Note: **15,000 gauss is the criterion of the MASTER CHRONOMETER certification — it is not a general level for mechanical watches**, and not a level attributed to any other brand or model in the sources this article uses.

On the ISO 764 standard: the ISO 764:2020 standard page on iso.org describes the document as one that "specifies the minimum requirements and test methods for magnetic resistant watches", applying to watches "designed to withstand daily magnetic fields". The standard's specific level is not stated in the public sources this article draws on, so no figure is quoted here.

## If a watch starts running oddly

A watch that suddenly drifts can have **many different causes**. From a single symptom, **do not conclude that the watch has become magnetised** — identifying the cause belongs to a qualified specialist.

Seiko describes the direction of the effect in its FAQ: an adversely affected watch "may gain or lose time" — it can run **fast or slow**, not only one way. For mechanical watches, Seiko is explicit: "If the hairspring gets magnetized, the oscillation of the balance will be unstable and this may cause a gain or loss" — a magnetised hairspring makes the balance's oscillation unstable, which can push the watch fast or slow.

- Consult the **manufacturer's documentation** for your watch first.
- Take it to a **qualified specialist** for examination — they identify the cause and the appropriate course of action. If magnetisation is suspected, Seiko's guidance is to have the watch demagnetized at a service centre ("contact the nearest Authorized Seiko Service Center to have the watch demagnetized") — demagnetization belongs to a qualified service, not a do-it-yourself step at home.
