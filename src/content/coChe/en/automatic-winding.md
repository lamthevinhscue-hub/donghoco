## The core idea

An **automatic** (self-winding) watch uses the movement of your wrist. The Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie describes the mechanism as one that winds the mainspring **by using the movement of the arm**: arm motion sets a swinging weight turning, and **through specific gears of the movement**, that motion is led back to winding the [mainspring](/en/glossary/mainspring/), where energy is stored.

## The rotor

The central part is the **[rotor](/en/glossary/rotor/)** — in FHH's definition, a **semi-circular disc that freely rotates with each movement of the arm** to wind the mainspring, while **its own weight returns it to a vertical position**. Because the rotor is heavy and mounted off-centre, everyday wrist motion is enough to turn it.

A **specific system of the calibre** multiplies the rotor's rotations to wind the mainspring continually. Which gears that system uses, and in which direction it leads the force, are design decisions of each calibre: two automatic watches need not share one structure.

## Where the energy goes

The wound mainspring stores its energy inside the [barrel](/en/glossary/barrel/). From there, energy is released through the familiar gear train of a mechanical watch, and how long it lasts is the watch's [power reserve](/en/mechanisms/power-reserve/).

## What winding in practice depends on

How much energy gets stored while you wear the watch depends on **several things at once**, so no general promise covers every watch:

- **How much the wearer moves** — the rotor needs arm movement to turn.
- **The calibre's design** — its gearing is the maker's decision.
- **The current state of the mainspring** — the fuller the power reserve, the longer the watch keeps running once set down.
- **Whether hand-winding is possible, and how** — that is specific to each model; the deciding source is the manual for your watch, and safe hand-winding is covered in [winding a mechanical watch](/en/guides/winding-a-mechanical-watch/).

So if your watch stops early despite being worn regularly, compare the manual for the model before guessing at a cause.

## When the watch is stored

Not worn, the rotor does not turn, the mainspring slowly relaxes, and the watch stops when the reserve runs out — that is normal behaviour, not a defect. Some people consider a watch winder to keep the watch rotating while stored: [watch winders](/en/guides/watch-winders/) is a neutral frame of questions for deciding whether the device suits your watch — that article neither recommends buying one nor advises against it.

## Summary

**Arm movement** → **the rotor turns freely** (its own weight returns it to vertical) → **the calibre's specific gearing** multiplies the rotations → **the mainspring in its barrel** stores energy → the gear train.

The structure and the direction of force are decisions of each movement — the surest way to know how your watch behaves is still the manufacturer's manual.
