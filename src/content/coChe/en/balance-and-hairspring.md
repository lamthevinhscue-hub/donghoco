## Two parts, one role

- The **balance wheel** — per FHH, a circular, moving part which **oscillates on its rotational axis**: a circular mass (rim) held by spokes.
- The **hairspring** (balance spring) — per FHH, a very fine spring, the **"heart" of a mechanical watch**; its ends are affixed to the balance and the balance-cock.

Combined with the spiral, FHH writes, the balance forms the watch's **regulating organ** — that is the exact role of this pair.

## Dividing time into equal parts

Per FHH, the spring gives the balance its to-and-fro motion, **through which it divides time into strictly equal parts**. Each to-and-fro movement — a "tick-tock" — is **one oscillation**, and each oscillation **comprises two vibrations**.

## What determines the swing

FHH states it directly in the *Balance spring* entry: **the length of the spring and the balance's moment of inertia determine the duration of each oscillation**. And because the spring's length can be altered, changing it is how a watch is **regulated**. The spring's elasticity is what allows the balance to keep making regular oscillations — each swing winds the spring, and the spring pulls the balance back, over and over.

## Longer or shorter — which way it goes

Horopedia describes the direction of the length effect with a guitar-string comparison: the greater the working length of the hairspring, the lower the oscillation frequency; the shorter, the higher. The same encyclopedia reads that direction straight onto the watch: one that runs fast has its regulating organ oscillating at too high a frequency, and one that runs slow, too low. Correction follows the same relationship — bringing the spring-holding part of the regulator closer to the stud increases the active length, lowers the frequency, and the watch tends to lose time; moving it away shortens the active length, raises the frequency, and the watch gains — with all other conditions unchanged.

"Active length" is worth keeping apart from the image of cutting a spring shorter or splicing wire onto it: it is the part of the spring that actually takes part in the oscillation, and its boundary is set by the position of the spring-holding part of the regulator — changed by turning the regulator, not by trimming or adding to the spring itself.

## Moment of inertia — why "heavier runs slower" isn't the whole story

Length is only half of the story — FHH names both factors: the spring's length and the balance's moment of inertia. Horopedia defines the moment of inertia as the physical quantity that measures a body's resistance to a change in its rotational speed around an axis. On the balance: the farther the mass sits from the axis, the greater the moment of inertia; the closer, the smaller — and at equal mass, the distribution around the axis is what decides. Direction of effect (other conditions unchanged): moving mass toward the inside of the balance decreases the moment of inertia and increases the frequency, so the watch runs fast; moving it outward increases the moment of inertia, lowers the frequency, and the watch runs slow. In other words, the deciding quantity is the moment of inertia, not mass alone — the shorthand "a heavier rim runs slower" doesn't hold on its own.

## The regulator — a common route, not the only one

That spring-holding part is the **regulator**. Per Horopedia, the regulator pivots concentrically around the balance's axis of rotation: a long arm to turn it and, opposite it, a short arm which — depending on the construction — carries two pins, one pin with a regulator key, or two pins with a key; the hairspring passes between the two pins, or between the key and a pin. The regulator's position determines the working length of the hairspring, which is why turning it changes the frequency.

Horopedia also states a construction limit: the regulator is not an essential component for a mechanical watch to operate. Some constructions use variable-inertia balances — masses adjustable inward or outward on the rim — and do without a regulator entirely, correcting through the balance's moment of inertia instead of through the spring's working length. The same encyclopedia notes the advantages of that route: it is less prone to being thrown off by a shock, and it removes the influence of pin-on-spring friction on [isochronism](/en/glossary/isochronism/).

## Who feeds the oscillator?

Per FHH, the **[escapement](/en/mechanisms/escapement/)** — fitted between the gear train and the regulating organ — suspends the gears' motion at regular intervals and **supplies energy to the balance**. The oscillator sets the pace; the escapement keeps it supplied.

Why does the feeding have to be continuous? At the general level of physics — a mass on a spring, not watches in particular — OpenStax describes how real-world oscillations rarely keep going: friction and other resistive forces remove energy from the system, the amplitude — how far each swing travels — gradually decreases, and eventually the motion stops. Notably, the same text states that with a small amount of damping the period and frequency stay nearly constant — what shrinks is the amplitude, not the pace. Supplying energy, then, is not about sweeping the balance faster or in a different rhythm; it replaces the energy the resistive forces take away, so the oscillation continues at the pace the regulating organ sets.
