## What you see

In its most common form — two pushers flanking the winding crown: press one pusher (START) and the **additional seconds hand starts running**; press it again (STOP) and the hand **stops instantly**, so you can read the elapsed seconds; press the other pusher (RESET) and the hand **snaps back to zero**. Those are the three phases of a chronograph: **Start → Stop → Reset**. The number of pushers and the layout vary by design — not every watch is arranged the same way.

A chronograph (from the Greek *chronos*, time, and *graph*, to write) is a watch that **measures short periods of time** independently of telling the time. It is one of the most common complications with a direct, practical use: FHH records that this mechanism came with many variants — the flyback was devised for pilots, while measurement scales such as the pulsometer, tachymeter, telemeter and slide rule were attached to chronographs for specific measuring needs.

## The principle — how a chronograph works

A chronograph is **not a second movement** running in parallel. It draws power from the main movement through a **clutch** that engages and disengages. Depending on the design, the clutch is of the **horizontal (lateral)** or the **vertical** type.

### Three operating states

The three phases repeat in a fixed cycle:

1. **Stopped** — the clutch is disengaged: the chronograph seconds wheel stands still, the hand does not move. The watch keeps normal time; only the stopwatch function is off.
2. **Running** — press START: the clutch engages, power flows from the main movement to the chronograph seconds wheel, and the hand begins to sweep. A counter wheel set (minutes, and on some watches hours) records the elapsed total.
3. **Reset** — press the reset pusher: a **hammer** strikes a **heart cam**, forcing the hand back to exactly zero. The heart-cam mechanism brings the hand back to 12 o'clock from whichever angle it stopped.

### Column wheel and cam — two ways to control it

The switching between the three states is governed by a central component. FHH records two varieties: the **column wheel — the traditional technique** — and the **cam-type system, a more rational one to manufacture**.

The **column wheel** is a small wheel carrying upright **pillars**. Each START/STOP press works like this:

- A **feeling pusher** advances the wheel by **one step**.
- **Levers** slide over, or drop between, the pillars:
  - Dropping into the **gap between two pillars** → the lever pulls the clutch into engagement → power flows → the hand runs.
  - Riding **on top of a pillar** → the lever pushes the clutch out of engagement → power is cut → the hand stops.

Beyond the control system, chronographs also differ in how they are attached to the movement: the mechanism can be **built into the movement**, or **mounted as an add-on module**. Per FHH, the integrated solution is considered nobler but involves greater development costs and less flexibility; the module is less expensive.

## History — measuring time, defining sport

**1816**: Louis Moinet built his "compteur de tierces" — a device reading sixtieths of a second, beating 216,000 vph (30 Hz), operated by two pushers, designed for astronomical observation. FHH regards it as the invention of the chronograph. **1821**: Nicolas Mathieu Rieussec — a watchmaker by appointment to the King of France — unveiled a device at a horse race, accurate to a fifth of a second, that **dropped ink** onto a rotating enamel dial to record the time; the name "chronograph" (Greek for "writing time") was born there. **Around 1861**: the modern chronograph with an additional hand appeared (Henri-Ferréol Piguet, on behalf of Nicole & Capt — per FHH).

The **20th century** was the golden age of the chronograph tied to speed: motor racing (the Heuer Carrera in 1963, the Monaco in 1969 — per TAG Heuer), aviation (the flyback — per FHH, devised for pilots), and space exploration (the Omega Speedmaster — [the watch worn on the Moon in 1969, per Omega](/en/iconic-watches/omega-speedmaster/)).

**1969** was the landmark year of the automatic chronograph: per FHH, the first self-winding chronograph movements were launched that year — the **Chronomatic** (developed by Dépraz-Bürgen for Breitling and Heuer-Leonidas, the basis of the Calibre 11 in the Monaco) and the **El Primero** by Zenith, the first to beat at 36,000 vph. That same year, per SEIKO, Caliber 6139 was introduced — "may have been the world's first such timepiece", as SEIKO itself records.

### Column wheel or cam, integrated or module?

One function, several ways to build it:

- **Column wheel** — the traditional technique; drives levers that drop between pillars or ride over them.
- **Cam-type** — a more rational system to manufacture, per FHH.
- **Horizontal or vertical clutch** — a matter of design; each type has its own operating advantages.
- **Integrated or module** — integration is considered nobler and costlier to develop; modules are less expensive.
