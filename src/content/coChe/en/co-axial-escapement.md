## The phenomenon — what you see

Some premium watches advertise a "co-axial escapement" as a major feature — yet looking into the movement, it is hard to spot the difference from an ordinary Swiss escapement. So where does the improvement sit, and why does it justify the money?

## The principle — the problem with the traditional escapement

### The Swiss lever: sliding friction

The [Swiss lever escapement](/en/mechanisms/escapement/) — the type used in nearly every mechanical watch — transfers force from the [escape wheel](/en/glossary/escape-wheel/) to the [balance](/en/glossary/hairspring/) by **sliding friction**. Two ruby jewels on the [pallet fork](/en/glossary/pallet-fork/) scrape along the escape wheel's teeth, kicking the balance at every beat.

The problem: **sliding friction is high**, so those two ruby jewels **must be lubricated with oil**. Without oil, friction rises, the rate drifts, and wear accelerates.

And here is the core issue: **oil degrades over time** — it thickens, migrates, evaporates, picks up dust. As the oil degrades, friction and error creep back. That is why a mechanical watch needs **periodic servicing** — largely to **replace the escapement's oil**.

### Co-axial: a push instead of sliding

The co-axial escapement (invented by George Daniels) attacks the problem differently: instead of letting the ruby jewel **slide** over the escape teeth, the tooth shapes and jewel positions are redesigned so that force transfers **almost perpendicularly** — a **push**, not a slide.

Pushing friction is **very small** compared with sliding friction. Therefore:

- **Less dependence on oil** — friction is low enough that oil is secondary, not essential.
- **Degrading oil matters less** — because the friction itself is small, oil in poor condition does not raise the error by much.

The result: a watch with a co-axial escapement **holds its accuracy longer between services** — potentially several years with an acceptable drift, where a Swiss lever needs its oil replaced every 3–5 years.

## Why doesn't everyone use it?

If the co-axial is better, why do most watches still run a Swiss lever?

- **Harder to manufacture** — the tooth geometry is complex, the tolerances tighter, production costs more.
- **Patents** — the co-axial design was held exclusively by one large Swiss brand for years, widening only later.
- **The Swiss lever is "good enough"** — with periodic servicing, the traditional escapement runs very steadily. The co-axial is an improvement at the top end, not an urgent one in the mid market.

## The historical significance

The co-axial escapement is the **greatest mechanical-watch invention of the 20th century** — not because it is everywhere, but because it improved the very part that had been treated as unchangeable for more than 200 years. The Swiss lever has dominated since the 18th century; hardly anyone thought it could be replaced. George Daniels proved otherwise.

One large Swiss brand commercialized the co-axial in **1999** — one of the landmark moments of modern mechanical watchmaking (see [the History page](/en/history/)). Since then the co-axial has spread gradually, though it remains a minority. It stands for the conviction that "the mechanical watch can still be improved" — against the idea that everything was settled back in the 18th century.
