## What are you hearing?

Press a running mechanical watch to your ear and you hear a steady **tick... tock... tick... tock...**. That is not "the hands moving" — it is the **escapement** at work. Modern watches beat faster than you might think: 28,800 vph (the Rolex/Omega standard) equals **8 beats per second**; 36,000 vph (Grand Seiko Hi-Beat) is **10 beats per second** — the individual ticks blur into a continuous hum.

The escapement is the **most important invention** in the history of mechanical timekeeping — without it, the mechanical watch could not exist.

## The problem the escapement solves

Picture the mainspring as a coiled store of energy. Released freely, it would **empty in seconds**. For the watch to run for hours, something must **divide** that energy into small, even beats. The escapement is that gatekeeper.

## Three moving parts

The Swiss lever escapement (the most common type today, descended from the detached lever escapement of Thomas Mudge in the 18th century) has three parts moving in sync:

1. **Escape wheel** — a special wheel with hooked teeth. It wants to spin (driven by the mainspring), but the fork holds it back.
2. **Pallet fork** — a Y-shaped lever with two **pallet stones** of synthetic ruby or sapphire. It rocks back and forth, alternately locking and releasing the escape wheel.
3. **Balance wheel** — the oscillating wheel, the heart of accuracy (see [hairspring & balance](/en/glossary/hairspring/)).

## One beat, step by step

1. The balance swings to one side and nudges the pallet fork.
2. The fork rocks → one pallet stone **releases** the escape wheel → it **advances exactly one tooth** (the "tick").
3. Immediately, the other pallet stone **locks** the wheel — no more energy escapes.
4. The balance swings **to the other side** → the fork rocks back → the other stone releases → the wheel advances another tooth (the "tock").
5. Repeat, forever.

With each release, a **small impulse of energy** reaches the balance — replacing what friction takes away, keeping the oscillation alive. It is an elegant **mechanical feedback loop**: the balance sets the pace, the escapement feeds it. The tick and the tock are the sounds of **pallet stones catching teeth**.

## Why ruby stones?

The **jewels** in the pallet fork and at the pivots are **synthetic ruby or sapphire** (red, transparent). The reasons:
- **Hardness** (9/10 on the Mohs scale) → minimal wear, lifetimes measured in generations.
- **Smoothness** → less friction, less lubrication needed.
- Before 1902 — when **Auguste Verneuil** invented the flame-fusion process for synthetic ruby — watches used natural stones at enormous cost. Today synthetic rubies make the movement affordable. Most simple three-hand movements use around 17 to 21 jewels; chronographs and multifunction movements often use more, some beyond 30.

The "21 jewels" printed on a caseback counts exactly these stones.

## A short history

The Swiss lever escapement has served for **more than 250 years** and remains the default — nothing has fully replaced it. Each major improvement was a small earthquake:

- **Around 1755**: Thomas Mudge invents the detached lever escapement — the foundation of the later Swiss lever. The widely used Swiss lever of today is the product of a long 19th-century refinement process, not Mudge's original design.
- **1999**: Omega industrializes George Daniels' **Co-Axial** escapement — the most significant departure in 250 years, reducing friction.
- **2001**: The Ulysse Nardin Freak brings **silicon** into the escapement — antimagnetic and running without conventional lubrication.
