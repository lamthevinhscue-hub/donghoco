## The phenomenon — what you see

Leave the watch under a lamp for a few minutes, then take it into a dark room: **the hands and hour markers glow green** — enough to read the time in darkness. This is **lume** — one of the most useful features a wristwatch can have, particularly in the days when electric light was new.

## The principle — two ways to glow

Watch lume glows in two different ways. On one glossary page the Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie splits it into two families: photoluminescent light (using energy absorbed from a light source) and radioluminescent light (using the radioactive property of the coating substance). **Phosphorescent materials** (phosphorescence) — the kind used today — absorb the energy of light, store it briefly and **release it slowly in the dark**; they must be "charged" by ordinary light (a lamp, the sun) and fade as their stored energy runs out. Nemoto describes its LumiNova pigments exactly this way: the pigments absorb energy from the sun or artificial lighting and emit light after the energy source is removed. **Radium and tritium**, used earlier, glowed continuously without charging: the radioactive substance constantly excited a phosphor mixed with it (radioluminescence).

Modern phosphorescent pigments come in different glow colours — green, blue, white.

## Three generations of luminous material

### Generation 1 — radium (abandoned)

In the early 20th century, **radium** — a radioactive element — was mixed into luminous paint: EPA records that in the early 1900s luminous paint was made with radioactive materials. Radium **glowed continuously**, no charging needed: the radioactive substance constantly excited the phosphor mixed into the paint — the element itself does not emit visible light. That was the great advantage at the time: a watch that glowed by day and by night without charging.

But radium is **highly radioactive** — EPA describes it as emitting alpha, beta and gamma radiation — and the price was paid by the workers. EPA records that many radium dial painters licked the bristles of their paintbrushes to a point; inside the body, radium behaves like calcium and settles into the bones. These workers later became known as "The Radium Girls". Their story is one of the earliest painful lessons in industrial occupational safety.

> This story is told at a level of **respect**, without detail of the illnesses. What matters is the lesson about responsibility towards the people doing the work.

For today's collector, EPA has practical advice: **do not take apart** old radium watches or dials; a dial that glows in the dark without a battery may contain radium or tritium.

### Generation 2 — tritium (much weaker)

RC Tritec tells this transition: until the **early 1960s** dials were still painted with radium zinc sulphide; then the highly radioactive radium was replaced by **tritium**, a weak beta emitter. mb-microtec describes tritium as a soft beta emitter whose electrons carry very low energy. Tritium also **glowed continuously** without charging: its radiation excites the phosphor mixed with it.

One current application of tritium is the tritium gas tube: mb-microtec describes a trigalight as a glass capillary coated on the inside with a luminescent zinc-sulphide powder and filled with **tritium gas** — the tube lights on its own, with no external energy source. NRC puts tritium's **half-life at about 12.3 years** — the time it takes for half of the undecayed tritium atoms to decay. The half-life is a property of the radioactive substance, **not** the service life of the glow in any specific product.

On older watches you can find **"T Swiss made T"** or **"Swiss T<25"** on the dial. As the FHH glossary describes it, these are optional markings for tritium-activated deposits: "T Swiss made T" corresponds to a tritium quantity emitting less than 227 MBq (7.5 mCi), and "Swiss T<25" to less than 925 MBq (25 mCi) — FHH names ISO 3157 as the frame of reference for these markings.

### Generation 3 — Super-LumiNova (modern, non-radioactive)

In **1993**, Nemoto created its series of long-afterglow pigments named **LumiNova**. RC Tritec manufactures Super-LumiNova in Switzerland and describes the material as **free of any radioactivity**. The principle differs completely from the two earlier generations: the material **does not glow by itself** — it must be **charged with light first** (a few minutes under a lamp), then it releases the light gradually in the dark, the same principle Nemoto describes for its LumiNova pigments.

Advantage: no radioactivity. Drawback: the glow fades over the hours, and recharging is needed for a strong light. Hence the rhythm of modern lume watches: "charge by day, glow by night".

## The historical significance

Lume was born from a **practical need**: reading the time in darkness before electric light. FHH notes that radioluminescence is generally reserved for professional-use watches — military and diving; EPA records that during World War II, radium dials and gauges allowed pilots to fly at night without cockpit lights. Lume was once a working tool for people who had to read the time in darkness, not a decoration.

Today, with electricity and phone flashlights, lume is less "life-and-death" — yet it remains one of the most highly rated features for **everyday usefulness**. A watch without lume is nearly unreadable in a cinema or at night — which is why lume remains a common feature on modern mechanical watches.
