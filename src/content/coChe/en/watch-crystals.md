## The phenomenon — what you see

The transparent layer covering the dial and hands is the **crystal**. At a glance the types look alike, but they differ markedly in hardness, brittleness and price — and that affects both the watch's durability and its looks.

## The principle — three crystal materials

### Acrylic

Acrylic crystal is **transparent, flexible plastic** — the classic crystal; every old watch used it.

- **Strengths**: **very flexible** — it does not shatter on impact (it dents or scratches instead); **light**; and most importantly — **it can be polished back** when scratched, with polishing compound and a soft cloth.
- **Weaknesses**: **it scratches easily** — a brush against a wall or a sleeve leaves a mark.
- **Character**: acrylic crystals are usually gently **domed**, a classic look.

### Mineral glass

Mineral glass is **ordinary glass**, heat-treated to be harder than window glass.

- **Strengths**: **much harder than acrylic** (harder to scratch) and **cheap** — found on most mid-market watches.
- **Weaknesses**: **more brittle than acrylic** — when it breaks, it breaks into sharp shards; it **cannot be polished** back like acrylic; a scratched crystal means replacement.

### Sapphire

Sapphire crystal is **synthetic sapphire** — the second-hardest material after diamond.

- **Strengths**: **practically unscratchable** — only diamond or another sapphire will mark it; **perfectly clear**.
- **Weaknesses**: **more brittle than even mineral glass** — a hard knock can crack it (though it hardly scratches); **expensive** — the priciest crystal type; and **reflective** — polished sapphire needs an added **anti-reflective coating**, usually on the inner face (sometimes both), to stay readable under lights.

## Why sapphire isn't always the best

This is the common misconception: assuming that sapphire, being the most premium, is always the best choice. In fact it depends on the purpose:

- **Vintage and military watches used acrylic for a reason**: acrylic **does not shatter** on impact — it dents. In a military environment (falls, knocks), a crystal that does not fragment protects both the dial and the wearer. A vintage watch wearing sapphire would **look out of place** — too glossy, too flat, missing the spirit of its era.
- **Deep-diving watches use sapphire**: underwater, knocks are rarer while scratch resistance matters more (sea sand, coral), and sapphire handles pressure well.
- **Budget watches use mineral glass**: the balance between hardness and cost.

So no type is absolutely "best" — there is only the type that best fits the purpose.

## The historical significance

Crystals went from acrylic (early 20th century) → mineral glass (mid-century) → sapphire (from the 1970s–1980s, as artificial sapphire became cheaper to produce). The shift reflects the progress of materials — but it also **changed the look of watches**: vintage acrylic domes softly with gentle reflections; sapphire sits flat, clear and modern.

Vintage collectors often **protect the original acrylic** rather than replace it with sapphire — the original crystal is part of the watch's authenticity. Fitting sapphire to a vintage watch is a form of over-restoration that costs collector value.
