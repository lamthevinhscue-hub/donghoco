## The short answer

Not necessarily. Jewels are small stones serving as **bearings**, **endstones** or **pallet-stones** in the movement, **reducing friction** at the turning pivots; in shock-protection systems they also help **keep oil** in place. The total jewel count tells you about a movement's **construction**; it does not by itself say whether the movement is good or bad.

## Why many people think so

On many dials or case backs, the jewel count is printed clearly — sometimes very large. Reading the number is the easiest way to compare two watches side by side, so it is easy to come to believe that more stones mean more value. The comparison passes from one buyer to the next, while the real work of jewels happens deep inside the movement and is rarely explained.

## What is actually true

### What jewels are for

Per the Stone entry of the Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie (FHH), in watchmaking a stone is used as a **bearing**, an **endstone** or a **pallet-stone**, and is known internationally as a **jewel**. The three names match three jobs: a bearing supports a turning pivot, an endstone closes against it axially, and a pallet-stone sits in the escapement's lever.

Incabloc, a maker of stones and shock-protection systems for the watch industry, describes the concrete purpose: the main purpose of a jewel is to **reduce friction between metal and stone**. Because jewels withstand relatively high pressure, they also help keep the pivots from binding.

### Jewels also hold oil — within the shock-protection system

In the Incabloc shock-protection system, the jewel works with the endstone to form an **oil sink** of a calculated shape and size, keeping the drop of lubricant centered on the pivot. Note the scope: this description belongs to the Incabloc system; not every jewel in every movement shares the same oil-holding construction. Where the oil sits, how much of it there is and how the endstone is cut all follow that system's design — the article does not extend the description to bearings outside it.

### How to read the jewel count

Each jewel serves a functional position: FHH lists the roles of bearing, endstone and pallet-stone — that is, at the pivots of the movement, in the escapement and in the balance. Movements with additional functions — automatic winding, for example, or other complications — often need extra bearing points, so their jewel counts run higher. In that case the number reflects the **construction of the movement**: it tells you which bearing points exist, not how well each one is made.

Comparing two movements by the total number of jewels alone therefore does not say which one is better. A movement's real quality lies in its design, materials, finishing and regulation — things the number does not touch.

The position of the stones also explains why counts differ between movements. A simple hand-wound calibre and a complicated automatic one support different numbers of pivots, so their jewel counts differ by design long before any question of quality arises. Reading the count as a construction summary — which functions the movement carries — matches what the sources actually describe.

## What cannot be concluded

- What the jewel count printed on a specific model means within that movement's design needs the movement maker's documentation.
- A movement's real quality also lies in its design, materials, finishing and regulation — things a jewel count does not reveal.

If you are weighing a watch, treat the jewel count as information about construction — then look into what kind of movement it is, what its maker documents about it, and how it is regarded by people who have worked on it.
