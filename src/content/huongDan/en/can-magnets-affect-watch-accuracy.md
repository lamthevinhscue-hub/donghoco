## The short answer

Yes, if the field is strong enough. Oris writes that a Swiss watch movement **can become magnetized, affecting accuracy**. Makers advise keeping a watch away from **speakers, refrigerators, magnetic bag clasps and tablet covers**. Some watch lines carry anti-magnetic certification with a published threshold.

## Why many people think otherwise

Electronic devices are everywhere, so it is easy to imagine that everything is a "strong magnet". Some people worry excessively; others dismiss magnetism as a myth. The reality sits in between: some everyday objects do produce significant fields, but not every device is alike — and the makers' own advice draws the line where the field is strong, not where electronics merely exist.

## What is actually true

### A mechanical movement can be magnetized

FHH defines an anti-magnetic watch as one **protected against magnetic fields**. Protection is needed because, as Oris notes, a movement can be magnetized and **lose accuracy**. A magnetized watch can run far further from its normal rate — even though nothing appears visibly broken.

### The objects makers advise avoiding

In its usage guidance, OMEGA advises keeping the watch away from strong magnetic fields, **such as those of speakers, refrigerators, certain bag clasps or tablet covers**. The list is worth remembering because these are familiar objects: a bag with a magnetic clasp, a tablet case closed by a magnet, or a watch resting on top of a speaker.

### Anti-magnetic watches have specific thresholds

Resistance is stated as a number, not a vague promise:

- METAS, the Swiss metrology institute, certifies watches under the **Master Chronometer** programme; the METAS-N001 requirement document states a threshold of **1.5 tesla (15,000 gauss)** for magnetic-field resistance.
- OMEGA describes the Master Chronometer programme — whose criteria are set by METAS — as 8 tests over 10 days, during which the watch is exposed to a **strong magnetic field of 15,000 gauss**; those are the certification's test results, and the maker still advises avoiding strong fields.
- Oris publishes that Calibre 400 **deviates by less than 10 seconds per day after exposure to 2,250 gauss**, exceeding ISO 764.

These three examples belong to specific product lines. An ordinary mechanical watch does not automatically carry such levels — and even on a certified watch, the published figure describes what the certification tested, not a promise about every magnet it may ever meet. Note also what the figures describe: a laboratory test exposure followed by a measured deviation, not a guarantee for every field a watch may meet in daily life.

The difference between the lines matters as well. One certification tests at 15,000 gauss, one movement is published at 2,250 gauss — each number belongs to its own programme and its own movement, and none of them transfers to a watch that carries no such certification.

## What cannot be concluded

- **Does a mobile phone affect a mechanical watch?** OMEGA lists phones among the examples of magnetic sources to avoid; that is not enough to conclude that every phone, at every distance, affects every watch.
- **Is your watch magnetized right now?** Do not conclude from a faster rate alone — a rising rate error has many possible causes, and magnetism is only one of them. Record the rate for a few days, check the maker's documentation, and take it to a qualified service point for testing.

If in doubt, the least costly step is observation: [tracking watch accuracy](/en/guides/accuracy-tracking/) explains how to record day-by-day readings. A consistently one-directional deviation is data to hand to a watchmaker, not a final conclusion. Demagnetizing is a workshop procedure; the sources in this article give no home demagnetizing method, and this article sets out none.
