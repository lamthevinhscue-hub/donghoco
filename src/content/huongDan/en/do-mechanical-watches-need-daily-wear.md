## The short answer

No. An automatic mechanical watch **winds itself from the motion of your wrist** while you wear it. Left unworn for a few days, its power reserve drains; for **models with a hand-winding mechanism**, the maker advises **winding by hand** per their instructions. A watch that has stopped because its mainspring ran down is not broken — wind it, set it, and it runs again.

## Why many people think otherwise

Two opposite beliefs circulate. Some think an automatic watch "winds itself forever" and are surprised to find it stopped after a few days in a drawer. Others worry that letting a watch stop is harmful, so they wear it every day or buy a winder. Both come from not knowing where an automatic watch gets its energy.

## What is actually true

### Energy comes from the wrist and is stored in the barrel

Per FHH, automatic winding **uses the motion of the arm to turn a rotor**, which winds the mainspring through gearing. The rotor is a semicircular disc that swings freely with every movement. The mainspring sits inside the **barrel**. Some models also have a separate hand-winding mechanism — as the Seiko manual below notes — in which case the crown winds the watch by hand, following the maker's instructions.

OMEGA describes it the same way: an automatic watch is **powered by the natural motion of the wrist**.

### What happens after a few days unworn

Per OMEGA, if the watch is not worn for several days, or wrist motion is not enough for optimal winding, **the power reserve may fall below its maximum** — and the maker advises hand-winding in that case.

Seiko gives concrete figures per movement:

- Calibres **8R46 and 8R48**, fully wound, run **about 45 hours**; the manual also notes that running time can be shorter when the chronograph is used a lot. Seiko advises: when the watch is off the wrist, wind it fully **once a day at a fixed time**.
- Calibres **5R65 and 5R66** (Seiko's Spring Drive) reach a full mainspring when worn **12 hours a day for 3 to 5 consecutive days**. The two examples come from two different technologies — neither is a rule for every mechanical watch.

The two examples show that running time and wear needs **differ by movement** — from the same maker, Seiko, two calibre families already have different wear requirements and different running times. Experience gathered on one watch therefore does not transfer automatically to another. For yours, consult the guide for that exact model.

A stopped automatic watch is also not an emergency. The movement has not lost anything by resting; wind it again as the maker instructs and set the time and date following that model's date-setting guidance rather than by force. The rest is routine — wear it, or wind it, and the movement takes over from there. Nothing about a mechanical watch requires it to be on the wrist every single day; what it requires is energy, however that energy arrives.

### What about a winder?

OMEGA recommends a winder from its own accessory line for watches left unworn. That is one maker's recommendation for its own products. Whether a winder suits your watch needs judging model by model — see [Watch winders](/en/guides/watch-winders/).

## What cannot be concluded

- The fully-wound running time of your movement differs by calibre — check the maker's instructions.
- Whether a winder helps or harms in the long run: the sources in this article do not settle it.

The safe routine: if the watch rests for a few days, letting it stop is fine; when you wear it again, wind it as the maker instructs, then set the time and date following that model's date-setting guidance (see [Setting the date safely](/en/guides/setting-the-date-safely/)).
