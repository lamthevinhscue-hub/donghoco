Finishing terms come up in almost every description of a mechanical movement, and they are easy to read for more than they say. This guide stays with three common ones — [guilloché](/en/glossary/guilloche/), [perlage](/en/glossary/perlage/) and [Côtes de Genève](/en/glossary/geneva-stripes/) — and states what each describes per its source, plus the limit: these terms describe surface treatments, not a verdict on how the watch runs, where it came from, or what it is worth.

## 1. What movement finishing is — within the source's scope

The Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie (FHH) defines finishing as **the final operation in a process** (per FHH). In FHH's description of movement decoration, parts receive different surface treatments (per FHH):

- polishing in several forms, producing different surface reflections (per FHH);
- brushing, which striates the metal with fine lines; satin finishing, which produces a softer sheen (per FHH);
- stippling (spotting), which forms a pattern of overlapping concentric circles (per FHH);
- sunray decoration radiating from a central point; snailing, spirals around a central point (per FHH);
- edges of parts polished and chamfered (per FHH).

At the standards level, the Poinçon de Genève publishes its requirements **per group of components** — for baseplates, plates for additional mechanisms and bridges specifically, with the general aim that machining marks be eliminated (per the Poinçon de Genève). Finishing is therefore not one vague idea: for each type of component, the published requirement for that component can be checked.

## 2. Which surface treatment each term describes

**[Guilloché](/en/glossary/guilloche/)** — per FHH, a style of engraving **by hand or by machine** with intersecting wavy or straight lines; the guilloché effect results when the workpiece moves **horizontally or vertically** against the tool, distinguished from flinqué/flinking, which follows a radial movement (per FHH). The definition describes a technique and a line pattern — not the skill of the person who made a given part, and not its value.

**[Perlage](/en/glossary/perlage/)** — FHH describes **stippling (spotting)** as forming a pattern of **overlapping concentric circles**, and records parts as "circular-grained" (per FHH). In the Poinçon de Genève's published requirements for baseplates, plates for additional mechanisms and bridges, the recesses must receive a "perlées" (circular-graining) finish — **or another finish** that likewise eliminates all machining marks (per the Poinçon de Genève). The standard ties this finish to specific component locations and to the aim of eliminating machining marks, not to an overall quality level.

**[Côtes de Genève](/en/glossary/geneva-stripes/)** — per FHH's glossary, a decoration of **undulating lines, like waves**; FHH's encyclopedia article names Côtes de Genève as one example of evenly-spaced, parallel lines, straight or circular, achieved with a boxwood pad and a lathe on the surfaces of larger parts such as bridges (per FHH). In the Poinçon de Genève's published requirements, **the tops of bridges** must be finished with Geneva stripes — **or any other decoration** that eliminates machining marks (per the Poinçon de Genève). Again, the standard leaves room for several decorations that meet the same stated aim.

## 3. Why a term or a photo shouldn't be read as a verdict on performance, provenance or value

Four reasons, all drawn from the sources above:

- **The same pattern can be produced by different processes.** FHH describes guilloché as engraving "by hand or by machine", and Côtes de Genève as achieved with a tooling process (a boxwood pad and a lathe) — so the term alone does not reveal how a given part was made (per FHH).
- **The standard does not tie overall quality to one pattern.** The Poinçon de Genève requirement for bridge tops reads "Geneva stripes or any other decoration that eliminates machining marks" — the published aim is eliminating machining marks, not one specific decoration (per the Poinçon de Genève).
- **A photo does not state the process.** A photograph shows a surface pattern at a moment in time; it does not state which technique produced it, nor any operating figures of the movement.
- **Performance, provenance and value belong to other kinds of information.** Performance is established by measurement and testing; provenance is documented per model by its maker; value is a transaction property. None of these is contained in the definition of a finishing term.

So the three terms in this guide should be read the way their sources state them: **names of surface treatments**. They are not certifications, and on their own they are not an assessment of the watch as a whole.

## 4. When you need specifics — check the right document

- For a specific model, the most direct source is the **technical documentation published by its maker for that model**.
- For standards, check the **rules published by the organisation concerned** — for example, the Poinçon de Genève publishes finishing requirements separated by component group: baseplates, plates for additional mechanisms and bridges in one group; shaped parts and supplies such as screws and pins in another (per the Poinçon de Genève).
