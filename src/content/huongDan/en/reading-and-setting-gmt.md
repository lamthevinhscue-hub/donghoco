> A [GMT](/en/glossary/gmt/) watch shows two time zones at once. Reading is the easy part — setting takes care, because how a movement is set is **a property of each calibre**. This guide covers the display principle; real operation always follows the manufacturer's documentation for the exact model.

## Three parts to understand

1. **The hour hand** — shows local time (where you stand), one turn per 12 hours, like any watch.
2. **The GMT hand** — shows the time in another zone (usually "home time"), completing **one turn per 24 hours**. Distinguished by its own design or colour.
3. **The 24-hour scale** — usually on the outer bezel; on **some models** the bezel is rotatable and is used to read an **alternative time zone**.

## How to read it

- **Local time** — read the hour hand as on any watch.
- **Home time** — see which number the GMT hand points to on the 24-hour scale (note: the GMT hand runs on a 24-hour scale — on "6" it is 06:00, on "18" it is 18:00; morning or evening is already in the number).

## Setting it — follow the model's own documentation

Which hand can be set, and how, is a property of each calibre — **there is no universal procedure**. Read the documentation for the exact model before adjusting; do not pull the crown and experiment on guesses.

One sourced example: **the Rolex GMT-Master II** — from the 1982 movement, the local hour hand sets **in one-hour jumps via the winding crown**, without affecting the minute hand, the seconds hand, or the 24-hour hand; the GMT hand keeps the reference time. Other calibres are set differently — that is exactly why the model's own documentation comes first.

## Reading an alternative time zone with the rotatable bezel

Only models with a **rotatable 24-hour bezel** can use the following principle:

1. Turn the bezel by the **time-zone difference** you want to track (how far ahead or behind the other city is).
2. Read the **GMT hand against the rotated bezel scale** — the number at the hand's tip is that alternative zone's time.

While the bezel serves the alternative zone, the **reference time is no longer readable directly off the bezel** (Rolex describes exactly this for the GMT-Master II); return the bezel to its neutral position — the triangle aligned with 12 o'clock — to read it again. There is no universal direction or counting formula shared across models; the detailed operation follows the model's documentation.

## When is a GMT useful?

- **Flights across time zones** — home time for calling people at the right hour, local time for living your day.
- **Working with people abroad** — you know their working hours before you schedule a call.
- **Family far away** — call when they are awake, not in the middle of their night.

## Common mistakes

- **Mistaking the GMT hand for the hour hand** — the GMT hand is boldly coloured, and beginners assume it is the main hand. Remember: the slender hand is the hour hand; the coloured one is GMT.
- **Forgetting the GMT hand runs on a 24-hour scale** — the GMT hand pointing at 6 is not "6, morning or evening, whichever"; it is **6 o'clock** absolutely, on a 24-hour scale.
- **Setting by guesswork** — each calibre is set its own way; guessing can leave the hands mislinked. Read the documentation for the exact model before adjusting.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a GMT watch a dual time zone watch?**
Yes — GMT is a form of dual time zone display (local + reference). Some models add a rotatable 24-hour bezel, which can display one alternative time zone more.

**How is GMT different from a world timer?**
A world timer shows many cities' times at once (usually on a ring of city names) with no per-zone setting. A GMT shows a second zone — plus, on some models, an alternative zone via a rotatable bezel — and is simpler.

**Why does the GMT hand take 24 hours per turn?**
So that one revolution of the hand equals one full day — every position corresponds to one specific hour of the day, with no repetition (unlike a 12-hour hand, which repeats twice daily). That is what makes home time unambiguous, day and night.
