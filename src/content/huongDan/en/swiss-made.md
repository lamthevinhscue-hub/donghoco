> This article explains what the "Swiss Made" label means and does not mean, based on Swiss legislation and official bodies — not shopping advice.

## The common misconception

The most frequent question about this label is also where the misunderstanding lives: **Swiss Made does not mean the watch was made entirely in Switzerland.** The label is an **indication of source** under a Swiss federal ordinance, with a specific cost threshold — not a promise that every component was produced domestically. A Swiss Made watch can perfectly well contain legally imported components.

## Seven criteria that must hold simultaneously

Under the ordinance currently in force (checked directly on Fedlex, 20 September 2026), a watch may carry "swiss made" on its dial only if **all** of the following conditions hold:

| Criterion | Content |
|---|---|
| **Technical development** | The core technical elements of the watch must be developed in Switzerland — for purely mechanical watches, the mechanical construction and prototyping of the watch as a whole |
| **Swiss movement** | The movement must be a Swiss movement |
| **Movement development** | The movement's technical development must also take place in Switzerland |
| **Movement assembly** | The movement is assembled in Switzerland |
| **Movement inspection** | The movement is inspected by the manufacturer in Switzerland |
| **Movement costs** | At least 60 per cent of the manufacturing costs of the movement are incurred in Switzerland |
| **The complete watch** | Casing-up of the movement and final inspection take place in Switzerland; at least 60 per cent of the manufacturing costs of the complete watch are incurred in Switzerland |

For the movement, the ordinance adds a value condition: components manufactured in Switzerland must represent at least **half of the value** of all movement components (excluding assembly costs).

## Why 60 per cent

The 60 per cent threshold is not an industry convention — it is the result of the **2017 revision of the ordinance**. The Swiss Federal Council adopted the partial revision on 17 June 2016, in force from 1 January 2017, tightening the indication of source in line with the general "Swissness" legislation. Before that milestone, the percentage requirement applied only to the movement, not to the complete watch.

## Two legal milestones to remember

- **1971** — the original ordinance on the use of the name "Swiss" for watches came into force.
- **1 January 2017** — the partial revision took effect, narrowing the gap between the label and the reality of production.

## Smart watches are covered too

A detail few pages mention: the ordinance's definition of a "watch" **has been extended to include smart watches** — per the Swiss Federal Institute of Intellectual Property (IGE), following new technology. The ordinance is therefore not just a historical document.

## What the label says — and what it doesn't

- Swiss Made **says** where the development, production, assembly and final inspection took place — under the legal conditions above.
- Swiss Made **does not say** anything about accuracy, finishing quality or a "better" ranking. Those belong to separate certifications and standards — for example [`chronometer`](/en/glossary/chronometer/) or [`METAS Master Chronometer`](/en/glossary/master-chronometer/) — each with its own scope and criteria, separate from the indication of source. The Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry (FH) likewise states that these criteria are "defined by law" — a legal scope, not a quality scale.
