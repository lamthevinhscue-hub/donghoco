> A [tachymeter](/en/glossary/tachymeter/) scale is a series of numbers engraved around the watch — on the bezel, on the rehaut or on the dial, depending on the model — on many [chronograph](/en/glossary/chronograph/) watches. It reads the **average speed over a fixed distance** — and it is not intuitive to use. This guide walks through the method step by step.

## The method, step by step

1. **Prepare** — establish the distance you will measure. OMEGA's definition uses a 1000-metre distance, read as km/h; if a watch's scale is calibrated for miles, the same principle reads mph over one mile — each scale belongs to its matching distance unit. You need a start point and an end point exactly that distance apart.
2. **Start** — when the object (a car, a runner) passes the start point, **press the start pusher** of the [chronograph](/en/glossary/chronograph/). The seconds hand begins to run.
3. **Finish** — when the object covers the distance, **press the stop pusher**. The seconds hand stops.
4. **Read** — look at the tachymeter number the hand is pointing to. That is the **average speed** (units per hour) over the distance you measured.

Example: a car takes 30 seconds to cover 1 km. The hand stops at 120 on the scale — an average of **120 km/h**.

## Why the scale is not evenly spaced?

This is a signature of a real tachymeter scale, and a common source of confusion. The scale is **not evenly spaced** — the marks at the beginning **sit close together**, the marks at the end spread **further and further apart**.

The reason: on a standard scale — one calibrated for a single unit of distance (1 km) — the arithmetic behind it is **speed (km/h) = 3600 ÷ time in seconds to cover 1 km**. This follows from OMEGA's definition of km/h based on a 1000-metre distance.

Two different things must be told apart: the seconds hand sweeps the dial **uniformly with time**, while the speed values printed next to the marks do **not** change uniformly with that spacing. Because the arithmetic is a reciprocal division:

- **Short times → high speeds: the marks sit close together on the dial even though the speed values change sharply.** The hand sweeps uniformly — 5 seconds and 6 seconds are only 6° apart on the dial, so the 720 and 600 marks sit side by side, even though the values differ by 120 km/h.
- **Long times → low speeds: the marks sit farther apart while the speed values change by less.** 50 seconds and 55 seconds are 30° apart on the dial, so the 72 and 65 marks sit far apart, even though the values differ by only about 7 km/h.

In other words: the **spacing of the marks follows time (uniform)**, while the **speed values next to them change at a varying rate** — do not confuse the two. The high-speed region has its marks placed close together; the low-speed region has its marks placed farther apart. It is not a defect — it is the geometry of a reciprocal division.

## The limits of the scale illustrated here: 60 to 400, corresponding to 9–60 seconds

The scale illustrated in this article runs from **60** to **400**, corresponding to measured times from **9 to 60 seconds**:

- **Under 9 seconds** — shorter than the highest number of the illustrated scale, so it cannot be read directly.
- **Over 60 seconds** — the seconds hand has completed a full revolution and repeats its position, so the scale no longer reads directly.

A tachymeter scale on a real watch may cover a different range — 9–60 seconds is only the limit of the scale illustrated in this article.

With that range of times, the tachymeter scale is typically used for **vehicle speeds and measurements with a similar pace**; slower activities usually fall outside the numbers printed on the scale.

## What else can a tachymeter measure?

The same principle works for **any quantity expressed in "units per hour"**, as long as you know what one unit is:

- **Production** — time one unit of output; the number reads as units per hour.
- **Frequency** — time one cycle; the number reads as occurrences per hour.

Just substitute "km" with the matching unit.

## Common mistakes

- **Pressing at the wrong moment** — starting or stopping early or late relative to the distance markers skews the result. The pusher must be pressed exactly as the object passes the marker.
- **Forgetting the scale assumes exactly one unit of distance** — if your distance is 2 km, the reading must be **multiplied by 2** (the scale is calibrated for 1 km).
- **Measuring something slower than the scale's range** — once the elapsed time passes the lowest number printed on the scale (over 60 seconds on the illustrated scale), it no longer reads directly. A result is only valid while the time stays within the range of the scale on that model.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does a tachymeter need a chronograph?**
To read one directly on a watch, you need a hand or device that measures elapsed time — a chronograph is a common way to provide it, but a scale on its own cannot measure time. Without a timing hand to read against, there is nothing to read off the scale.

**Where is the tachymeter placed?**
It depends on the model: on the bezel, on the rehaut (the inner edge of the dial) or printed on the dial itself. How you read it depends on the scale and the timing hand of the exact model.

**Do I actually need a tachymeter?**
Most wearers will not use it daily. But understanding the principle helps you evaluate a fully functional chronograph — and see why the scale is not evenly spaced. To see how the timing side works in detail, read how the [chronograph mechanism](/en/mechanisms/chronograph/) works.
