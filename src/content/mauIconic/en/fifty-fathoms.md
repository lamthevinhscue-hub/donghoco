## Origins

In 1952, two French naval officers — **Robert Maloubier** and **Claude Riffaud** — were tasked with founding the French navy's combat swimming school. They needed a **professional** dive watch for covert underwater missions. They drew up the technical requirements and sent them to several brands — most declined, calling the brief "unreasonable".

Only **Blancpain** said yes. Two years later (1953), the brand introduced the **Fifty Fathoms** — a professional dive watch built to that set of requirements. The Rolex Submariner arrived the same year, 1953.

## Side by side with the Submariner — two paths to the same year

The Fifty Fathoms and the [Rolex Submariner](/en/iconic-watches/rolex-submariner/) were both born in 1953, from different starting points:

- **Fifty Fathoms** — born from the technical brief set by the French military divers; according to Blancpain, the brand's "first modern dive watch".
- **Submariner** — according to Rolex, the first dive watch water-resistant to 100 metres (as documented in our Submariner article); the earliest reference 6204 examples are dated to late 1953, with the model presented at Basel in 1954.

Sharing a birth year **says nothing about who copied whom** — 1953 was simply the moment the professional dive-watch idea ripened on both sides of the Atlantic, much as the diving chapter of [our history timeline](/en/history/) frames it. A detailed comparison has to go reference by reference; at the level of horological history, the two deserve to be read side by side: each contributed its own path to the dive-watch standard that followed.

## Why "Fifty Fathoms"?

**One fathom = 1.8288 metres**. Fifty fathoms = **91.4 metres** — the depth Maloubier calculated as the maximum a diver of the era could descend on the tanks available without dying. That figure became a benchmark for dive watches in the years that followed.

## Design signatures

- **A unidirectional rotating bezel** — present on the Fifty Fathoms from the 1953 generation, later to become the dive-watch standard.
- **A curved bezel** — rounded rather than faceted, a signature look.
- **A black dial** with large round indices under a thick layer of lume.
- **An acrylic crystal (Plexiglas)** — it does not shatter under deep pressure.
- **Sporty yet elegant** — never as utilitarian as the modern diver.

## Jacques Cousteau and "Le Monde du Silence"

In 1956, filmmaker **Louis Malle** and oceanographer **Jacques-Yves Cousteau** produced the documentary *"Le Monde du Silence"* (The Silent World) — winner of the Palme d'Or at Cannes. In the film, Cousteau's dive team **wears the Fifty Fathoms**. The film carried the Fifty Fathoms to the world.

## Reference generations

- **50 Fathoms** (1953) — the original, water-resistant to 91m.
- **MIL-SPEC** (1957) — the American military version, with a moisture indicator.
- **Bathyscaphe** (1956) — a smaller version, designed around the Trieste submersible's dive to the Mariana Trench.
- **500 Fathoms** (2009) — a larger version (48mm), water-resistant to 1000m.

## The movement

The modern Fifty Fathoms runs the in-house calibre **1315** — automatic, three barrels, 120 hours of power reserve (a weekend-and-more reserve), silicon antimagnetic. It is widely considered one of the **most beautiful** dive movements in the industry (usually seen through the sapphire caseback).

## Its place in history

The Fifty Fathoms' historical role is **on par with the Submariner's**, though far less famous with the general public. Where the Submariner became a pop-culture symbol, the Fifty Fathoms kept the standing of the **"professional diver's watch"** — tied to combat swimmers, professional divers and the history of the tool watch.
