## Origins

In 1976, Patek Philippe — a name known for elegant dress watches — unexpectedly launched a **steel sports** watch. It was a response to the trend the Audemars Piguet Royal Oak had started in 1972. **Gérald Genta** (the same designer behind the Royal Oak) was given the brief, and he sketched the Nautilus in under five minutes.

## Design signatures

- **A porthole-shaped case** — inspired by a ship's porthole, with the brand's distinctive hinged "ears" secured by screws.
- **A horizontally lying bezel** — unrounded, giving the case an almost rectangular, softly edged silhouette.
- **A blue-black dial** with the signature horizontal embossing — shifting tone depending on the viewing angle.
- **An integrated bracelet** — flowing as one piece with the case, a Gérald Genta hallmark.

## The legendary scarcity

Patek Philippe is known for its policy of **deliberately limiting production** — making fewer watches than the market demands to preserve exclusivity. The result: the Nautilus (especially the steel ref. 5711) was always scarce on the secondary market, trading far above retail. When Patek announced the end of the 5711 in 2021, the collecting world erupted.

## Reference generations

- **3700/1A** (1976–1990) — the first generation, the 42mm "Jumbo", extremely rare.
- **3800/1A** (1981–2006) — a smaller 37.5mm version, with a date.
- **5711/1A** (2006–2021) — the generation that redefined the Nautilus for the modern era, the most sought-after.
- **5811/1G** (2022–present) — the 5711's successor, in white gold for the first time.

## The movement

The current Nautilus runs the in-house calibre **26-330 S C** — automatic, with a date, around 45 hours of power reserve, finished to the Patek Philippe Seal standard.

## Its place in history

The Nautilus is more than a watch — it is **a symbol of success**. Together with the Royal Oak, it created an entirely new segment: **steel luxury sports** — one that A. Lange & Söhne and Vacheron Constantin would later pursue as well.
