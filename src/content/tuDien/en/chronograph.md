A **chronograph** (from the Greek *chronos*, time, and *graph*, to write) is a watch with an added **stopwatch function** — you measure a short period of time (seconds, minutes) independently of telling the time, using one or several pushers, usually on the side of the case.

The name was born with Nicolas Mathieu Rieussec's device (1821) — unveiled at a horse race, it truly "wrote" by dropping ink onto a rotating dial to record the time ("chronograph" = "writing time").

## How to recognize it

- **Pushers** next to the winding crown — the number and layout vary by design; the most common form has two (one for start/stop, one for reset).
- **Sub-dials** on the dial — typically running seconds of the stopwatch, elapsed minutes, and possibly elapsed hours. The layouts vary widely from model to model.

## Chronograph, stopwatch, chronometer — what is the difference?

- A **chronograph** is a time-telling watch with an added stopwatch function.
- A **stopwatch (timer)** — per FHH — is an instrument that only measures time, not associated with a watch as such.
- A **chronometer** is not about the stopwatch: it is a movement whose precision is certified to an official standard. A chronograph can also be chronometer-certified — but not every chronometer has a stopwatch.

## Why so popular?

The chronograph is a complication with a direct everyday use: timing cooking, parking, running, racing... That is why it stands out among sports watches (Speedmaster, Navitimer...).
