**Incabloc** is the name of **one specific shock-protection system** made by Incabloc SA of La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland — it protects the **balance staff** of a mechanical movement. The Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie (FHH) defines this kind of structure (a shock absorber) as *"a resilient bearing to dampen shocks on the balance-staff pivots"*.

FHH's encyclopedia also notes: *"The majority of watches today use the Incabloc® system"* — according to FHH. But "the majority" is not "all": the name Incabloc is **not a synonym** for shock protection in general.

## The mechanism — as the maker describes it

Incabloc SA's technical page describes its system as follows:

- The **endstone** and its **bushing** sit inside a **bearing block**.
- The **double-cone director** brings the endstone and bushing **back to centre**, keeping the direction of travel **perpendicular to the balance staff** — in the maker's words, "pivots are therefore not subjected to any stress".
- The **lyre spring** acts as the positioning and return force, reacting instantly to shocks.
- The system works in **two phases**: the shock energy is entirely absorbed by the bearing block, then the system immediately returns to the exact centre through the action of the lyre spring.

The figures the maker publishes: a drop onto the floor from a height of about one metre corresponds to an acceleration of **around 5,000 g**, and the system's **reaction threshold** is **around 35 grams** — per Incabloc SA's description of its own system.

## The history, as published by the maker

- **1928** — Fritz Marti begins research into protecting watches against shocks; he builds a watch with a shock-absorbing system "with movable balance jewels".
- **1929** — The first Swiss patent (No. 141098) is registered.
- **1931** — Porte-Echappement Universel SA is founded in La Chaux-de-Fonds (renamed Portescap in 1963).
- **1933** — Production of the Incabloc® begins; the name Incabloc® is registered as a trademark on **6 July 1933**.
- **1938** — Final development: the lyre spring becomes the symbol of the system.

## Telling whether a watch uses Incabloc

The shape of the mechanism seen through the caseback is **not certain proof**. The only reliable way is to check **that model's specification or documentation** — the manufacturer publishes which shock-protection system its movement uses.
