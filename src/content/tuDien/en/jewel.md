A **jewel** is a **small stone set into a movement as a bearing for a wheel pivot**. The FHH encyclopaedia describes watchmaking stones as "used as a bearing, endstone or pallet-stone, known internationally as jewels", whose role is "to minimise friction".

## The principle

A wheel's pivot turns inside the hole of a stone instead of directly inside a metal hole — with less friction. This is exactly the role FHH states: jewels are "bearings for pivots to reduce friction".

As for the material, FHH places ruby and sapphire in the **corundum** family, describes a precious stone as "a hard and rare crystalline mineral", and notes: **"The majority of stones (jewels) used in watches today are synthetic."**

## The trap of the number

Many people assume "more jewels means a better watch". **That is a misconception.** The jewel count reflects the **movement's structure**: a movement with more wheels and mechanisms (a chronograph, a calendar) needs more bearings and therefore often carries a higher count. The figure is **not a scale** of quality, accuracy or durability, and it is not a buying recommendation.

FHH states in its encyclopaedia: "The movement of a quality watch has between **15 and 21 jewels**". Read that range as a **common level, not a ranking** — being inside or outside the range does not by itself say anything about quality; it follows the architecture of each movement.

## What the count tells — and what it does not

- **It tells:** how many stone bearings the movement uses — a piece of information about the movement's **architecture**.
- **It does not tell:** the quality of finishing, timekeeping accuracy, durability or value. Two movements with the same jewel count can still differ widely in all of those — judging them means looking at finishing, accuracy certification ([chronometer](/en/glossary/chronometer/)) and the maker behind the movement, not counting jewels.
